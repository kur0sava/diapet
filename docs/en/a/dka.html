Diabetic Ketoacidosis (DKA)

We know — even the name sounds scary. But understanding DKA is your superpower. The more you know about it, the faster you can help your cat.

What Is DKA in Simple Terms

When there isn't enough insulin in the body, cells can't absorb glucose from the blood. They "starve" even though blood sugar is sky-high. So the body starts breaking down fat for energy instead.

The byproduct of fat breakdown is ketones. In small amounts, they're harmless. But when too many ketones build up, the blood becomes acidic. That's ketoacidosis — a life-threatening condition.

Warning Signs — Memorize These

Here's what to watch for every day:

  • Vomiting — especially repeated, not a one-time event
  • Complete loss of appetite — your cat hasn't eaten for more than 12 hours
  • Severe lethargy — your cat just lies there and barely responds to you
  • Acetone breath — a sweet, fruity smell, similar to nail polish remover
  • Rapid, heavy breathing — deep, labored breaths that are easy to notice
  • Dehydration — dry gums, skin on the scruff slowly returns to place

If you notice two or more of these symptoms — act immediately.

When to Rush to the Clinic IMMEDIATELY

Don't wait until morning. Don't hope it will "pass on its own." DKA is an emergency.

Call your vet or drive to the emergency clinic if:

  • Vomiting + refusal to eat
  • Acetone smell + lethargy
  • Your cat can't stand up or is wobbling
  • Loss of consciousness or seizures

Every hour counts. The sooner treatment starts, the better the outcome.

What Happens at the Clinic

Don't worry — vets know exactly how to treat DKA. Here's what typically happens:

  1. IV fluids — restoring hydration and electrolytes
  2. IV insulin — fast-acting insulin through a drip
  3. Potassium correction — potassium drops dangerously during DKA and must be replenished
  4. Constant monitoring — glucose, electrolytes, ketones are checked every 2-4 hours
  5. Finding the trigger — DKA is often triggered by an infection or another underlying illness

Treatment usually takes 2-5 days in the hospital.

Why DKA Happens

DKA most commonly develops when:

  • Diabetes hasn't been diagnosed yet (first episode)
  • Several insulin injections were missed
  • The insulin dose has become insufficient
  • Your cat developed an infection
  • A pancreatitis flare-up occurred
  • Severe stress

Home Ketone Testing

Ketone test strips (Ketostix, Uriket) are a simple way to check ketones at home:

  1. Collect urine in a clean container
  2. Dip the strip for 1 second, read the result after 15 seconds
  3. "Trace" (+/−): be alert, increase monitoring
  4. "+" + feeling unwell: vet today
  5. "++" / "+++": emergency clinic

Important nuance: strips detect acetoacetate but NOT β-hydroxybutyrate (the main ketone in DKA). A negative result does not rule out DKA! If your cat is unwell — go to the clinic regardless of the strip result.

How to Prevent DKA

  • Never skip injections — if you're unsure the dose went in, it's better to skip than inject twice
  • Watch their appetite — refusal to eat for more than 24 hours = vet visit
  • Regularly measure glucose at home
  • Keep ketone test strips — check urine if glucose stays above 14-15 mmol/L (per O'Brien 2010 / ISFM 2023 — ketogenesis can already occur at this level; waiting for 20 is too late)
  • Don't skip vet appointments

Most Importantly — Don't Panic

With timely treatment, most cats fully recover from DKA. Yes, it's serious. Yes, it's frightening. But you can handle this, and so can your cat. You're not alone in this.
