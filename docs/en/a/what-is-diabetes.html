What Is Diabetes in Cats?

Basics · 5 min

What Is Diabetes Mellitus in Cats?

If your cat has just been diagnosed with diabetes, we understand how scary this feels. Take a deep breath. Thousands of cats live long, happy lives with diabetes, and you can handle this too.

Diabetes mellitus means your cat's body can't properly use sugar (glucose) from food. Either the pancreas doesn't make enough insulin, or the body's cells stop responding to it the way they should.

Types of Diabetes

Type 1 (Insulin-Dependent)

The pancreas produces almost no insulin. This is rare in cats. It requires lifelong insulin injections.

Type 2 (Non-Insulin-Dependent)

Here's the encouraging part: this is the most common type in cats (80-90% of cases). The body still makes insulin, but the cells don't "hear" its signals. And here's what's truly inspiring — with proper treatment, many cats achieve remission!

Who Is at Risk?

Some factors increase the chances of diabetes. This is not your fault — it's just helpful to know:

  • Extra weight — the most common factor
  • Age over 8 years
  • Male cats are affected roughly twice as often
  • Sedentary lifestyle
  • Steroid medications
  • Breed — Burmese cats have a genetic predisposition
  • Chronic pancreatitis

What to Watch For

Here are signs that might point to diabetes. If you notice any of these, it's time to visit the vet:

  • Frequent litter box visits — the box fills up noticeably faster
  • Excessive thirst — your cat drinks more than usual, seeks water in unusual spots
  • Weight loss even though appetite is normal or increased
  • Lethargy — your kitty has become less active and playful
  • Hind leg weakness — your cat walks with a lowered, "sinking" posture in the back
  • Eye cloudiness — rare in cats, more common in dogs

How Is It Diagnosed?

A single high glucose reading is not enough — clinic stress alone can push a healthy cat's blood sugar to 16–20 mmol/L. Diagnosis requires a combination of findings:

  1. Characteristic clinical signs (polyuria/polydipsia, weight loss with normal or increased appetite)
  2. Persistent hyperglycemia — confirmed on repeat testing (not a single measurement)
  3. Glucosuria — sugar in the urine
  4. Elevated fructosamine — reflects average glucose over 2–3 weeks and is not affected by momentary stress (see the dedicated article)

Only the combination of these factors distinguishes true diabetes from stress hyperglycemia. Specific numerical thresholds are for the vet to determine — don't self-diagnose from a single reading.

Good to know: cats are champion stress-reactors. The stress of a vet visit alone can temporarily spike blood sugar to 20 mmol/L even in a healthy cat! That's exactly why fructosamine is so important.

What Happens Inside the Body?

When there isn't enough insulin, a vicious cycle begins:

  1. Glucose from food can't get inside the cells
  2. Cells "starve" even though there's plenty of sugar in the blood
  3. The body starts breaking down fat for energy, producing ketones
  4. In severe cases, this leads to ketoacidosis — a dangerous condition that needs urgent care

But you can prevent this from happening! Consistent treatment and your loving care are the best protection.
